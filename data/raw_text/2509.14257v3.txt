Student-Centered Distillation Narrows
the Agentic Gap Between Small and Large LLMs
Yuanjie Lyu 1 Chengyu Wang 2 Jun Huang 2 Tong Xu 1
Abstract
Large Language Model agents achieve strong per-
formance on multi-step reasoning and tool-use
tasks, but their impressive capabilities typically
rely on extremely large backbones. Existing distil-
lation approaches train smaller students to imitate
full teacher trajectories, yet reasoning and knowl-
edge gaps between the teacher and student can
cause compounding errors. We propose SCoRe,
a student-centered framework in which the stu-
dent generates training trajectories and the teacher
corrects only the earliest error, producing train-
ing data matched to the student’s abilities and
exposing specific weaknesses.
The student is
first fine-tuned on corrected trajectories. Sub-
sequently, short-horizon reinforcement learning
starts from the verified prefix preceding the ear-
liest error, with target rewards assigned at that
step. This design enables the student to solve prob-
lems through unconstrained RL exploration rather
than teacher imitation, while the short-horizon
setup improves training stability. On 12 challeng-
ing benchmarks, a 7B-parameter student distilled
with SCoRe closes the agentic performance gap
with a 72B-parameter teacher1.
1. Introduction
Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have
led to the rise of “agents” (Xi et al., 2025). Unlike traditional
single-pass generation, LLM agents solve complex prob-
lems through an iterative reasoning–action–observation
loop, using frameworks such as ReAct (Yao et al., 2023)
and CodeAct (Wang et al., 2024). Specifically, LLM agents

decompose tasks into sub-goals (reasoning), execute them
via external tools such as code interpreters (action) (Schick
et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023), and then refine their plans
based on feedback from tool execution (observation). By
combining LLM planning with the precision of external
tools, agents mitigate flaws of LLMs such as hallucinations
and outdated knowledge, achieving strong performance on
real-world interactive tasks (Liu et al., 2024).
High-performing agents typically use large backbones
such as GPT-series (Achiam et al., 2023), resulting in
high latency and cost, as complex tasks may require
dozens of model calls. To reduce deployment cost, Agent
Distillation (Kang et al., 2025) decomposes the teacher
model’s behavior into structured [Thought, Action,
Observation] trajectories and trains a smaller student
model to imitate them. This enables the student to acquire
part of the teacher’s reasoning ability while reducing cost.
However, this imitation faces two main challenges: (1) Rea-
soning Ability Gap: smaller models often fail to reproduce
the teacher’s logical decomposition (Magister et al., 2023);
(2) Knowledge Capability Gap: the student may be unable
to execute complex actions despite following the teacher’s
plan. Both gaps stem from the emergent abilities (Wei et al.,
2022) of LLMs, gained through extensive training and not
fully transferable to smaller models. Moreover, in behavior
cloning, a single failure at any step can push the student into
out-of-distribution states, where errors compound and grow
as O(H2) with horizon length H (Ross et al., 2011).
To address the limitations of the traditional “teacher-acts,
student-clones” distillation paradigm, we introduce SCoRe
(Student-Centered One-step Reinforcement). In SCoRe,
the student first produces a complete trajectory, which the
teacher reviews globally, intervening only to correct the
earliest error. The student then restarts the task, resuming
from the original correct prefix and the teacher’s correction.
As shown in Figure 1, this student-centered approach to data
generation offers two key advantages: Capability Match-
ing, where trajectory complexity aligns with the student’s
abilities, keeping the data learnable and effective; and Defi-
ciency Localization, where the structure of a “verified prefix”
followed by a “key step” explicitly pinpoints the student’s
weaknesses for targeted optimization.

Before exploration, the student must first acquire basic
reasoning–action skills. We initialize it with a cold-start
Behavior Cloning (BC) phase, performing supervised fine-
tuning (SFT) on a small set of high-quality teacher trajec-
tories. Then, the core phase, Mentored Problem-Solving
(MPS), allows the student to tackle new tasks independently.
After the student completes a trajectory, a teacher inspects
it, identifies and corrects the earliest error, and the student
regenerates the remainder starting from this corrected pre-
fix. If further mistakes occur, the teacher repeats this pro-
cess. Final task success implicitly verifies the correctness
of the teacher’s fix2. We retain these corrected trajecto-
ries with minimal teacher intervention and use them for
the next round of SFT. By correcting the earliest error, this
approach limits teacher–student distribution shift to a sin-
gle step, breaks long error chains of BC, and yields O(H)
cumulative error growth rather than O(H2).
Although effective, these steps still rely on teacher correc-
tions and keep the student in imitation mode. To promote
genuine problem-solving and even enable the student to
surpass the teacher, we introduce a reinforcement learning
(RL) phase featuring shorter rollouts and key-step rewards.
Our RL method introduces two main innovations. First,
rather than rolling out from the start of the task, we be-
gin from the verified prefix preceding the student’s origi-

nal error. This shortens the horizon and reduces gradient-
estimation variance, making updates more stable. Second,
to mitigate the issue of sparse rewards (Andrychowicz et al.,
2017), we supplement the final task-success reward with
additional bonuses at key steps: a larger one for reproducing
the teacher’s correction and a smaller format reward for
avoiding the original error. Finally, during the MPS phase,
we retain the data that remains unsolved despite multiple
one-step corrections. A small part of this data is marked as

Experiments on 12 challenging benchmarks show that
SCoRe enables small models, such as Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct,
to achieve tool-use performance comparable to that of a 72B
teacher, outperforming both BC (Torabi et al., 2018) and
GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) by a large margin. These results
demonstrate that ability-matched correction, combined with
short-horizon key-step RL, can close the performance gap
between small and large models.

paradigm for training LLM agents in dynamic, multi-turn
environments (Shridhar et al., 2021; Mialon et al., 2024).
Early pipelines relied on supervised or rule-based tool-
use strategies (Schick et al., 2023), limiting adaptability
to new domains.
Later work integrated RL into agent

planning with local, verifiable corrections for stable learn-
ing and fine-grained reasoning control.

3. Background
We formalize the LLM-agent framework and review two
training methods: Behavior Cloning and Reinforcement
Learning, highlighting their limitations in agent distillation.
The ReAct Framework. To enable effective interaction
with an external environment E (e.g., a code interpreter
or a search engine), we adopt the ReAct framework (Yao
et al., 2023). In this framework, the agent’s behavior is
represented as a trajectory τ = (t1, c1, o1, . . . , tH, cH, oH),
where H is the total number of steps. At each step i, the
policy π first generates a thought ti based on the history si,
and then, conditioned on this thought and history, produces
a ReAct action ci:
si = (t1, c1, o1, . . . , ti−1, ci−1, oi−1),
(ti, ci) ∼π(· | si).
Executing ci in E returns an observation oi, and (ti, ci, oi)
is then added to the history. The loop continues until a
terminal action (e.g., producing the final answer) ends the
trajectory.
Agent Distillation via Behavior Cloning. To reduce the
inference cost of large LLM agents, agent distillation trans-
fers capabilities from a powerful teacher πE to a smaller
student ˆπ. The most common method, Behavior Cloning
(BC) (Torabi et al., 2018), trains ˆπ on teacher-generated tra-
jectories DT to imitate both thoughts (ti) and ReAct actions
(ci):

4.1. Initial Distillation with Code as Action
To enable a “student-explores, teacher-corrects” strategy
for agent distillation, the student must first acquire ba-
sic reasoning-acting skills. However, some LLMs (e.g.,
Qwen2.5 (Yang et al., 2025), LLaMA3 (Grattafiori et al.,
2024)) are not built for multi-step agentic tasks and typi-
cally perform single-pass generation. Therefore, we first
construct structured datasets and distill ˆπ to perform multi-
step reasoning and acting.
Trajectory Representation. Training trajectories follow the
ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) format: sequences of triplets τ =
(σi)H
i=1, σi = (ti, ci, oi), where ti is textual reasoning, ci is
an action in ReAct, and oi is environment feedback. These
structured trajectories capture problem decomposition and
stepwise solutions, making them well-suited for distillation
purposes. We build on CodeAct (Wang et al., 2024), where
ci is executable code, offering: (i) deterministic operations
for reproducibility; (ii) both the teacher and the student are
familiar with code from pre-training, reducing capability
gaps; and (iii) a Turing-complete, unified action space for
tool use and complex logic.
Initial Trajectory Generation. To build the initial training
data DT , we use the teacher policy πE. Following (Kang
et al., 2025), πE is first prompted to produce a high-level
plan <first thought> as a strategic outline. A second
prompt enforces the standard Thought–Code–Observation
cycle: at step i, πE generates (ti, ci) conditioned on both
the plan and prior steps. Executing ci in the environment
yields an observation oi, which is appended to the context
for subsequent reasoning. This dual-prompt design com-
bines global planning with adaptive step-level reasoning. To
ensure quality, we apply rejection sampling and retain only
trajectories with correct final answers.
Behavior Cloning. In the initial cold-start learning phase,
we distill the student policy ˆπ on DT via behavior cloning,
minimizing LBC as defined in (1). The resulting initialized
model, ˆπinit, learns the Thought–Code–Observation loop.
This capability enables ˆπinit to attempt tasks without imme-
diate failure, thus allowing fine-grained, one-step teacher
interventions in subsequent phases.
4.2. Mentored Problem-Solving and Further SFT
After initialization via BC, the core phase, Mentored
Problem-Solving (MPS), employs the BC-initialized student
as an explorer, producing ability-matched and deficiency-
localized trajectory data.
Student Explores, Teacher Corrects. As shown in Fig-
ure 2(a), the initialized student model ˆπinit attempts unseen
tasks. For each task, it independently generates a full tra-
jectory τS = (σ1, . . . , σH), where each step σi denotes a
triplet of Thought–Code–Observation. After generating the
final answer, the teacher πE checks correctness using the
student’s full reasoning chain. If the answer is incorrect, the
teacher locates the first deviation step σk where the student
diverges from the correct path. Instead of discarding the
trajectory, πE provides a minimal intervention by replacing

4.3. RL Refinement for Mastery
Although MPS yields capability-aligned supervision, train-
ing the student on this data only via SFT still limits the
student to replicating the teacher’s key steps. To move from
imitation to independent problem-solving, we introduce an
RL phase after SFT that directly optimizes task success. We
adopt GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), a variant of PPO (Schulman
et al., 2017) that omits the value function, thereby reducing
computation and value-estimation instability. However, for
long-horizon tasks, GRPO remains sensitive to sparse re-
wards and high gradient variance. As Figure 2(c) shows, we
address these issues with two techniques:
Short-Horizon Rollout. Instead of starting rollouts from
the initial question, we begin from the verified prefix
(σ1, . . . , σk−1): the sequence preceding the original er-
ror step σk. This shortens the horizon from H to H′ =
H −(k −1), reducing variance.

5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate our approach on three categories of
datasets. Mathematical Reasoning: AIME2024, AIME2025,
MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2023; Hendrycks et al., 2021),
and OlymMath (Sun et al., 2025). These problems often
require multi-step reasoning and frequent use of a code
interpreter. Factual Reasoning: HotpotQA (HQA), 2Wiki-
MultihopQA (2Wiki) (Yang et al., 2018; Ho et al., 2020),
Musique (MuSiQ) (Trivedi et al., 2022), and Bamboogle
(Bamb) (Press et al., 2023). These tasks demand multi-step
information retrieval and synthesis via search APIs. For
Deep Search, which is fully absent from the training data,
we follow WebThinker (Li et al., 2025c)’s text-only split
and test on GAIA (Mialon et al., 2024), WebWalker (Wu
et al., 2025), Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE) (Phan et al.,
2025), and xBench (Chen et al., 2025). These benchmarks
involve much longer tool-use horizons, making them more
challenging than standard reasoning tasks.
Baselines. We compare SCoRe with the following three
categories of baselines:
(1) Prompt-Only Large Model Agents:
strong LLMs
prompted in a tool-integrated reasoning (TIR) format (Li
et al., 2025b), without parameter updates. This setting re-
flects an approximate upper bound for performance under
ideal prompting. We include DeepSeek-V3 (671B)
and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct.
(2) Behavior Cloning (Kang et al., 2025)4: the student im-
itates full teacher trajectories, with no exploration or cor-
rection. Note that SCoRe uses the same total number of
training tasks as Behavior Cloning in Table 1 and 2.
(3) Trajectory-Level RL Algorithm: training the student with
RL over the full horizon with sparse task-completion re-
wards, using the GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) or ARPO (Dong
et al., 2026) algorithm. Note that RL baselines are also
warm-started with BC, rather than trained from scratch.
We also evaluate stronger teacher-guided baselines in Ap-
pendix D, including a PRM-style step-reward baseline, on-
policy distillation, and ReLIFT (Ma et al., 2026); these
baselines use richer supervision and higher training cost.
Our proposed SCoRe is evaluated in two sequential phases:
(i) SCoRe-SFT, a correction-based SFT phase where the
model is trained on capability-matched and deficiency-
localized data; and (ii) SCoRe-RL, a short-horizon RL phase
with rollouts from the verified prefix and key-step rewards
for reproducing the teacher’s fix or avoiding errors.
Evaluation.
To match the evaluation settings of
ARPO (Dong et al., 2026), we measure open-domain QA
tasks using a token-level F1 score against the ground truth.
For mathematical reasoning and deep search tasks, correct-
ness is judged by Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct by compar-

Implementation. During training and inference, the model
employs two tools: a Python code interpreter for math-
ematical reasoning and precise computation, and an on-
line search engine providing concise, up-to-date snip-
pets.
To save time and cost, we did not use a web
browser in deep search tasks. SFT training data follows
the [Thought-Action-Observation] format with
Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct as the teacher. To construct
the training data, we collect seed question–answer pairs
mainly from the Tool-Star dataset (Dong et al., 2025), in-
cluding NuminaMath (Li et al., 2024) and Omni (Gao
et al., 2025) for math reasoning, and HotpotQA, 2Wiki-
MultiHopQA, and WebWalker for factual QA. Of these,
20% of the trajectories are fully teacher-annotated to cre-
ate high-quality BC initialization data, giving the student a
solid foundation in reasoning–acting skills. The remaining
80% are generated via MPS, producing capability-matched,
deficiency-localized data. These minimally corrected tra-
jectories are split evenly: half for correction-based SFT to
produce the SCoRe-SFT model, and half for RL. Note that
during MPS, a substantial portion of the data can be solved
correctly by the student without any teacher intervention.
We retain only a small subset of these easy cases for training.
During RL training, the maximum number of rollout steps
was set to 8, and any reasoning–action cycles exceeding
this limit were considered incomplete. The same config-
uration was used during inference. For the key-step re-
ward scale Rkey, we set it to 0.5 based on a small-scale
2k-sample search over {0.3, 0.5, 0.7} on math benchmarks,
where 0.5 achieved the best performance. For the format re-
ward Rformat, we use the standard value of 0.1. Additional
details are provided in Appendix C.

SCoRe-SFT outperforms behavior cloning under the
same data budget. With only teacher-annotated trajec-
tories, BC performance is limited. Under the same data
budget, SCoRe-SFT yields consistent gains across back-
bones on both reasoning and deep search, showing that
MPS-constructed data reduces compounding error growth
from O(H2) to O(H), where H is the horizon length. This
also shows that even without RL, training on correction-
based trajectories substantially outperforms BC distillation.
RL with short-horizon and key-step rewards drives
strong performance. On the more challenging Deep Search
tasks (Table 2), SCoRe-RL achieves very strong performance
and in some cases exceeds the teacher. For Qwen3-8B
(Non-thinking mode), it scores 30.5 (+7.7 over BC, +8.3
over GRPO). This is expected: unlike imitation learning,
the learning signal in RL comes not only from the teacher
but also from the environment. Since the teacher provides
only local corrections rather than an optimal policy, SCoRe-
RL can explore beyond the teacher’s strategies, achieving a
large reward Rfinal (as described in Section 4.3).
Although the RL baseline is competitive, SCoRe-RL remains
better. Moreover, this advantage is not simply due to extra
teacher guidance or compute: in Appendix Table 8, SCoRe-
RL also outperforms stronger teacher-guided baselines such
as PRM-style step rewards and on-policy distillation.
5.3. Ablation Study
In the ablation study, we evaluate the contribution of each
SCoRe component on reasoning benchmarks (Table 3). The
BC-initialized student (Initial Distillation), trained on a sub-
set of high-quality teacher trajectory data, serves as the ex-
plorer for MPS. Unlike the BC baselines in Tables 1 and 2,
which use the full teacher data, this model performs poorly.
In contrast, when the initial model is treated as the explorer
and fine-tuned on data generated via MPS, performance
improves substantially (SCoRe-SFT), showing that training
on such data effectively reinforces weak links in the reason-
ing chain. Extending to RL brings further improvements:
(1) Without short-horizon rollouts, stability degrades and
performance drops notably, showing that truncation reduces
gradient-estimation variance; (2) Without key-step rewards,
accuracy on multi-step tasks decreases, validating the need
for targeted, stepwise guidance beyond final-task rewards.
The full SCoRe-RL approach, combining both short-horizon
rollouts and key-step rewards, achieves the best results.
Although the full SCoRe pipeline requires extra compute
due to MPS, the ablations in Table 3 are compute-matched,
using the same MPS-generated data and the same GRPO
training pipeline as ours. Hence, the gains of the full design
are not simply a byproduct of higher training cost.

Limitations
Despite reducing compounding error, the method does not
eliminate it. The O(H) result should therefore be inter-
preted as a conditional theoretical characterization under
the stated assumptions, rather than as a guarantee that dis-
tribution shift is fully resolved in practice. The framework
also depends on teacher quality during MPS. A weaker
teacher may reduce the amount of usable training data, but
the validation mechanism typically filters out incorrect cor-
rections, so its effect is more directly on data yield than on
supervision quality.

A. Relation to Closely Related Work
Why traditional DAgger cannot be directly used in agent distillation: DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) was designed in 2011
for classical imitation-learning domains (e.g., robotic control), where (i) the state is well-defined and can be judged locally,
and (ii) expert labels are treated as reliable supervision. These assumptions break down for LLM agents that act through
long-horizon, partially observable, tool-augmented interaction.
Our SCoRe method evaluates the entire trajectory and identifies mistakes only after seeing the global trajectory, which is
necessary to correctly pinpoint where errors occurred. In contrast, DAgger forces the teacher to correct the agent at every
step, even though many actions reveal themselves as right or wrong only later through tool feedback or final success or
failure. This is suitable for traditional imitation learning but not for agent distillation. Moreover, the teacher model itself can
make mistakes or contain noise; DAgger fully trusts the teacher, whereas SCoRe automatically filters out teacher errors,
which is crucial.
Other Closely Related Work: SCoRe is related to prior correction-based methods such as LEMMA (Pan et al., 2025),
which identify an erroneous step and regenerate from that point. Its key distinction is that SCoRe remains student-centered,
which is also the main focus of this paper: after correction, the student itself continues the trajectory, and the resulting
verified prefix is further used for short-horizon RL. This strict student-centered design is required by our theory.
SCoRe is also related to PRM-style methods with step-level rewards (Zhang et al., 2025), but it does not train a separate
process reward model. Instead, it uses first-error localization on student-generated trajectories to define both rollout starting
points and localized rewards.

Reinforcement Learning. The SCoRe-RL phase performs short-horizon fine-tuning using the GRPO algorithm (Shao et al.,
2024) within the VERL framework (Sheng et al., 2025). We adopt a global batch size of 128, a PPO mini-batch size of 16, a
rollout size of 16, and a maximum response length of 4,096 tokens. Training is conducted for 3 epochs on 8×NVIDIA H20
GPUs, using 5,271 search trajectories and 5,639 math trajectories.
For factual reasoning datasets, the search tool is implemented via the Google Search API. To reduce tool-call latency
during both training and inference, we include only the textual snippets returned by the API as the [Observation]
content, omitting full browser navigation or long-form summarization. This design substantially reduces retrieval time while
preserving sufficient context to answer queries effectively.

D. Additional Experiment Results
More baselines in reasoning benchmarks. Table 7 compares CoT Distillation, Agent Distillation, and our proposed
SCoRe-RL, with all methods evaluated using the exact-match metric on math reasoning benchmarks, following (Kang
et al., 2025). CoT Distillation trains smaller models to replicate step-by-step reasoning traces generated by large LLMs via
Chain-of-Thought prompting. While simple and effective, its reliance on static reasoning limits generalization to novel
knowledge or precise computation and can lead to hallucinations. Agent Distillation (Kang et al., 2025) transfers both
reasoning and tool-use skills from LLM agents by imitating complete [Thought-Action-Observation] trajectories.
Its training process is similar to our BC baseline in the main experiments (Tables 1 and 2), but introduces additional
enhancements: a first-thought prefix to produce higher-quality trajectories and a self-consistent action generation strategy to
improve inference robustness.
Agent Distillation has been shown to outperform vanilla CoT Distillation by incorporating structured, agent-style reasoning
into the distillation process. SCoRe-RL further exploits correction-based trajectories and RL to deliver substantial per-
formance gains. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, SCoRe-RL attains an average score of 49.3, a +7.5 improvement over
Agent Distillation (41.8), with notable gains on challenging benchmarks such as AIME (+5.6) and OlymMATH (+4.6).
On Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, it raises the average score to 42.1, +5.0 over Agent Distillation (37.1). These results
demonstrate that SCoRe-RL enables small models to achieve significant improvements beyond existing distillation baselines,

More baselines in deep search benchmarks. We additionally implement three stronger baselines, and compare with them
on the deep search benchmarks.
First, we implement a PRM-style baseline (BC + GRPO (Step Reward)), where the teacher evaluates each Thought–Action–
Observation step following the general setup of Zhang et al. (2025). The reward is defined as the proportion of steps judged
to move toward the correct solution, providing denser teacher-guided supervision than binary task-success rewards.
Besides, we implement On-Policy Distillation (OPD) (Lu & Lab, 2025). Since OPD typically requires the teacher and
student to come from the same model family, for the deep search setting with a Qwen3-8B student we use the largest dense
Qwen3 model as the teacher. This teacher is slightly stronger than the main teacher used in SCoRe.
We also implement ReLIFT (Ma et al., 2026), an interleaved RL–SFT baseline originally proposed for reasoning tasks.
Since our setting involves agentic reasoning with tool use, the number of hardest samples, i.e., queries on which all rollout
attempts fail, is relatively small. To provide ReLIFT with sufficient online SFT data, we use twice the training data, with
seed question–answer pairs drawn from ToolStar, the same source as ours.
The results in Table 8 suggest that teacher guidance alone is not sufficient. The PRM-style baseline already provides
dense teacher supervision at every step, yet it still underperforms SCoRe-RL. As illustrated in Figure 1, SCoRe retains
teacher corrections only when they are validated by the student’s success, and further combines such verified guidance with
short-horizon RL. We believe this coupling of verification and localized policy optimization is a key advantage of SCoRe.
On-policy distillation also underperforms SCoRe. A likely reason is that it directly imitates teacher trajectories that may not
be well matched to the student’s current capabilities. Although this mismatch may be reduced with substantially more data
and compute as the student improves, under our training budget SCoRe remains more effective.
SCoRe-RL also outperforms ReLIFT. We suppose that short-horizon RL provides a more stable optimization signal in our
setting, whereas ReLIFT may require a larger training budget and more ReLIFT-style hard samples to achieve comparable
performance.
