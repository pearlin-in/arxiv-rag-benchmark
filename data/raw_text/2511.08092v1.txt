ABSTRACT
We challenge the conventional view of neural network prun-
ing as solely a compression technique, demonstrating that
one-shot magnitude pruning serves as a powerful implicit
regularizer for ASR. Using Whisper-small, we combine
gradient- and Fisher-based sensitivity diagnostics with tar-
geted, component-wise pruning.
This reveals architectural
asymmetries: decoder FFNs are pruning-fragile, whereas de-
coder self-attention and the last encoder layers contain redun-
dancy that, when removed, improves generalization. Without
fine-tuning, pruning 50% of decoder self-attention reduces
WER by 2.38% absolute (20.44% relative) on LibriSpeech
test-other; pruning the last four encoder layers at 50% instead
yields a 1.72% absolute (14.8% relative) improvement. Gains
persisted on Common Voice and TED-LIUM datasets. Be-
yond regularization benefits, our sensitivity-aware approach
enables more aggressive one-shot compression.
At 40%
sparsity, where established global pruning approaches catas-
trophically fail, our method preserves near-baseline accuracy.
This positions pruning as a first-class architectural design
tool: knowing where to prune is as important as how much to
prune.

1. INTRODUCTION
Over-parameterization in deep neural networks facilitates
training convergence but often introduces redundancies that
can impair generalization.
Conventional regularizers such
as dropout [1], weight decay [2], and batch normalization
[3] mitigate this through stochastic or parametric constraints.
Beyond these established techniques, we demonstrate that
architectural analysis can reveal specific over-parameterized
components where pruning itself acts as regularization.
While pruning has traditionally been studied as a com-
pression technique aimed at reducing computational cost [4,
5, 6], we provide empirical evidence that post-training one-
shot magnitude pruning without fine-tuning can improve ASR
performance. We identify redundant parameters using gra-
dient and Fisher-based sensitivity diagnostics, and validate
them through component- and layer-wise pruning experi-
ments. These diagnostics enable targeted pruning that can
yield meaningful performance gains and improve robustness
to unseen acoustic conditions. They can also guide aggressive
one-shot pruning and surpass standard pruning methods that
collapse at similar sparsity levels.
Our contributions are threefold:
1. An architectural sensitivity analysis that quantifies per-
component and per-layer tolerance to one-shot magni-
tude pruning.
2. A pruning-as-regularization procedure that leverages
sensitivity analysis to improve ASR generalization
across diverse acoustic conditions.
3. Aggressive one-shot compression that surpasses stan-
dard pruning methods by leveraging the same sensitiv-
ity insights to preserve accuracy at high sparsity.
2. RELATED WORK
Pruning for compression. Pruning was originally introduced
to reduce model size and computation cost [5, 6, 4]. Architec-
tural analyses in Transformers reveal redundancy in attention
heads [7, 8, 9], with surveys [10] showing magnitude pruning
matching sophisticated approaches in terms of compression-
accuracy trade-offs. Our focus differs: we leverage magni-
tude pruning primarily as a regularizer.
Pruning as regularization. Prior studies link pruning to
generalization. Bartoldson et al. [11] link immediate accuracy
drops to larger eventual improvements with continued train-
ing, while Jin et al. [12] attribute improvements to a combi-
nation of longer training and reduced model size. We extend
this perspective to post-training one-shot pruning in ASR, re-
porting improvements without any fine-tuning.
One-shot and sensitivity-based pruning.
The Lot-
tery Ticket Hypothesis [13] showed sparse subnetworks in
dense models, inspiring one-shot methods like SNIP [14],
GraSP [15], and SynFlow [16] that identify masks at initial-
ization via gradient/flow criteria. Second-order approaches
(WoodFisher [17], Group Fisher [18]) drive pruning decisions
by approximating the Hessian. Kwon et al. [19] introduced
post-training Fisher-based pruning without retraining. We ex-

tend these by applying both first- and second-order analyses
post-training to diagnose regularization opportunities.
ASR pruning.
Prior ASR pruning work emphasizes
on compression, including structured pruning [20], adaptive
multilingual pruning [21], and sparse models [22], whereas
we show that one-shot magnitude pruning can improve accu-
racy without fine-tuning.
3. METHODOLOGY
3.1. Theoretical Foundation: Pruning as Implicit Regu-
larization
Classical regularization augments the empirical risk with ex-
plicit penalties like ∥θ∥2
2 (weight decay) or ∥θ∥1 (lasso) [2,
23]. Magnitude pruning instead acts as implicit regularization
by directly removing parameters rather than penalizing them.
For a network with parameters θ ∈Rd and target sparsity ρ,
we retain the (1 −ρ)d parameters with largest absolute val-
ues, creating a pruned network fθM where M ⊂{1, . . . , d}
indexes retained parameters:
θM = {θi : i ∈M},
|M| = (1 −ρ)d.

This sparsification constrains the network to operate with a
sparse parameter subset. It preserves essential features while
eliminating parameters that potentially encode spurious cor-
relations, providing a deterministic alternative to dropout’s
stochastic masks.

(gradient-based) and (ii) second-order (curvature-based) di-
agnostics, complemented by (iii) empirical, component-wise
pruning sweeps.

Pruning-based empirical sensitivity
Beyond analytic diagnostics, we empirically assess prun-
ing sensitivity by measuring pruning-induced degradation.
Components that maintain or improve performance when
pruned reveal opportunities for implicit regularization. We
employ one-shot, unstructured magnitude pruning, ranking
each weight by its absolute value (magnitude) and remov-
ing the lowest-magnitude fraction for a target sparsity. We
prune each architectural component independently in both
Encoder and Decoder, as well as split into layer subsets
(early/middle/late layers).
For target sparsity and compo-
nent (c, ρ), we zero weights |θj| ≤τ(ρ, c), leaving all other
parameters unchanged. We vary ρ in 10% increments.
3.3. Model, datasets and metrics
We use the publicly released Whisper-small model [25], an
encoder–decoder Transformer (12 encoder + 12 decoder lay-
ers) evaluated with Whisper’s official beam search decoding
pipeline. Primary evaluation uses LibriSpeech test-clean and
test-other [26] to contrast acoustically clean vs. challenging
conditions; treating test-other as our primary reporting met-
ric due to its greater acoustic variability. To probe general-
ization, we additionally validate on Common Voice v15 En-
glish (diverse accents/recording conditions) and TED–LIUM
Release 3 (conference talks with reverberation) using sampled
subsets from their test splits.
We quantify ASR performance using word error rate
(WER) and character error rate (CER). Resilience to prun-
ing is measured through ∆WER on test-other; lower values
indicate higher resilience, while negative values indicate im-
proved performance. As a note: unstructured masks are not
exploited by dense inference kernels, hence real-time factor
(RTF) remains unchanged in our setting.

4.2. Architectural Sensitivity Analysis
4.2.1. Encoder vs. Decoder
Building upon the previous diagnostics, Figure 2 reports
WER on test-other when pruning (i) the full model, (ii) the
encoder only, or (iii) the decoder only. Whole-model pruning
shows a catastrophic failure region between 30–40% spar-
sity. Separate pruning of encoder and decoder reveals that
the decoder drives this failure: at 40% sparsity, decoder-only
pruning yields 30.11% WER, whereas encoder-only pruning
remains relatively close to baseline (12.49%). This asymme-
try is particularly counterintuitive given the parameter distri-
bution: encoder components comprise only 36.47% of model
parameters while decoder components account for 63.53%.
Despite having more parameters, the decoder proves less
tolerant to pruning than the encoder. Notably, encoder-only
pruning at 30% slightly improves accuracy (-0.57% WER),
suggesting that selective pruning can indeed induce beneficial
regularization.
4.2.2. Layer-wise breakdown
We further analyze resilience at the layer level. We prune
early (1–4), middle (5–8), and late (9–12) layer blocks at
50% sparsity (Table 1). In the encoder, parameters become
less critical with depth, suggesting that early layer parame-
ters may be crucial for feature extraction. Late layers (9–12)
tolerate 50% sparsity and improve test-other by 1.72% WER
(14.8% relative). The decoder exhibits a U-shaped profile:
early layers (conditioning on encoder context) and late lay-
ers (producing token distributions) are critical, while middle
layers show redundancy.

4.2.3. Component-specific pruning
Table 2 summarizes component-level pruning sweeps, re-
vealing architectural asymmetry in regularization capacities
across elements. Encoder FFNs remain robust and decoder
FFNs are highly sensitive to pruning. Attention mechanisms
exhibit the opposite pattern—encoder attention is fragile
but decoder self-attention tolerates aggressive sparsifica-
tion. Crucially, several components yield improvements when
pruned, acting as implicit regularizers. Decoder self-attention
at 50% sparsity delivers the largest gain on test-other: -2.38%
WER (20.4% relative performance improvement). Additional
gains include encoder FFN (-0.52% at 40%), decoder cross-
attention (-0.45% at 10%), LayerNorms (-0.63% at 10%),
and model’s final output projection (-1.10% at 20%).
These improvements may be attributed to three comple-
mentary mechanisms: (i) pruning removes weak weights that
mostly add noise; (ii) it trims redundant or conflicting atten-
tion heads, making attention more focused; and (iii) it slightly
reduces capacity, which helps prevent overfitting, similar to
dropout but without randomness.

gains above, the same architectural sensitivity analysis en-
ables safer one-shot compression by steering sparsity away

5. CONCLUSION
This work demonstrates that one-shot magnitude pruning
serves as a powerful implicit regularizer for ASR, challeng-
ing its traditional role as solely a compression technique. By
combining gradient-based and Fisher Information diagnostics
with component-wise empirical validation, we reveal archi-
tectural asymmetries: decoder FFNs exhibit higher sensitivity
than encoder modules, while decoder self-attention and late
encoder layers contain substantial redundancy. Most notably,
pruning 50% of decoder self-attention weights improves
WER by 2.38% absolute (20.44% relative) on LibriSpeech
test-other without any fine-tuning. Similarly, pruning 50% of
the last four encoder layers yields a 1.72% absolute improve-
ment. These regularization benefits persist across Common
Voice and TED-LIUM 3.
The same diagnostics enable sensitivity-aware one-shot
compression, demonstrating that understanding where to
prune is as important as how much to prune. Where stan-
dard global pruning collapses near 40% sparsity, our al-
location reaches 40.8% sparsity with near-baseline WER
(+0.20%) and improved CER (-1.06%), reducing parameters
(241→143M) and compute (4.55→2.77 GFLOPs).
The practical implication is clear: pruning should also be
treated as a first-class architectural design tool rather than
just a post-hoc optimization. While our Whisper-small re-
sults are model-specific, the sensitivity analysis methodology
is broadly applicable. Practitioners can apply this sensitivity
analysis framework to identify pruning opportunities in other
Transformer architectures to improve robustness to unseen
acoustic conditions while delivering efficiency gains with no
fine-tuning.
