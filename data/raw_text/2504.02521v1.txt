Abstract
Knowledge distillation has emerged as an effective strategy for compress-
ing large language models’ (LLMs) knowledge into smaller, more efficient
student models. However, standard one-shot distillation methods often
produce suboptimal results due to a mismatch between teacher-generated
rationales and the student’s specific learning requirements. In this pa-
per, we introduce the UNDO: UNderstanding Distillation as Optimization
framework, designed to bridge this gap by iteratively identifying the stu-
dent’s errors and prompting the teacher to refine its explanations accord-
ingly. Each iteration directly targets the student’s learning deficiencies,
motivating the teacher to provide tailored and enhanced rationales that
specifically address these weaknesses. Empirical evaluations on various
challenging mathematical and commonsense reasoning tasks demonstrate
that our iterative distillation method, UNDO, significantly outperforms
standard one-step distillation methods, achieving performance gains of
up to 20%. Additionally, we show that teacher-generated data refined
through our iterative process remains effective even when applied to differ-
ent student models, underscoring the broad applicability of our approach.
Our work fundamentally reframes knowledge distillation as an iterative
teacher–student interaction, effectively leveraging dynamic refinement by
the teacher for better knowledge distillation.

Introduction
Knowledge distillation has emerged as an effective strategy for compressing large language
models (LLMs) into smaller, more efficient student models (Hinton et al., 2015; Sanh et al.,
2020; Zhu et al., 2024). Initially, the primary approach involved aligning the student’s
outputs with the soft logits of the teacher, enabling the student to approximate the teacher’s
distribution. Recently, this paradigm has expanded to include chain-of-thought (CoT) rea-
soning, which seeks to instill smaller models with interpretable intermediate steps rather
than simply matching final outputs (Shridhar et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023;
Chenglin et al., 2024).
Despite these advancements, standard one-shot distillation methods frequently suffer from a
distributional mismatch: the rationales generated by the teacher often do not align well with
the student’s specific learning challenges (Agarwal et al., 2024; Adarsh et al., 2024). To over-
come this limitation, we introduce the UNDO: UNderstanding Distillation as Optimization
framework, an iterative distillation strategy that actively identifies and addresses the stu-
dent’s errors by continuously prompting the teacher to generate refined, targeted rationales.
In each iteration, the teacher’s generation and student’s responses are analyzed by the
teacher, leading to tailored explanations that directly address the student’s learning gaps.
This loop ensures that the training data becomes more personalized to the student’s specific
needs, instead of simply reflecting generic teacher behavior.
Our iterative approach is inspired by educational concepts such as scaffolding (Wood et al.,
1976) and formative assessment (Black et al., 2004), which highlight the importance of adaptive,
targeted feedback in effective learning. Similar to a skilled teacher who adjusts instruction
based on a learner’s struggles, our method dynamically identifies and addresses the stu-

Empirical evaluations conducted on challenging mathematical reasoning datasets, including

Related Work
Knowledge distillation
Extensive research has explored knowledge distillation since Hin-
ton et al. (2015) introduced the concept of training smaller student models using soft-label
outputs (soft logits) from larger teacher models. Early influential models such as DistilBERT
(Sanh et al., 2020) demonstrated substantial parameter reductions ( 40%) without significant
performance losses. Subsequent advancements, like MiniLM (Wang et al., 2020), further
refined this technique by focusing specifically on distilling the self-attention mechanism.
More recent works, including Shum et al. (2024) and Timiryasov & Tastet (2023), explored
selective training strategies by choosing essential samples from the teacher’s training set.
The integration of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning (Wei et al., 2022) into distillation has
sparked additional innovations. For example, Shridhar et al. (2023) trained student models
to explicitly replicate the teacher’s CoT reasoning steps. Similarly, Li et al. (2023) generated
diverse rationales for individual questions, while Chenglin et al. (2024) extended this by
incorporating multiple reasoning styles. Zhu et al. (2024) distilled mathematical reasoning
into structured equations and combined various reasoning modalities (text, code, equations).
Further, Luo et al. (2025) leveraged reinforcement learning to fine-tune student models.
Despite these advancements, most approaches remain one-directional, with teacher outputs
independent of student-specific needs. In contrast, our method explicitly addresses the
student’s learning gaps by encouraging teacher awareness and adaptation.
Surveys such as Gou et al. (2021) and Yang et al. (2024) have also comprehensively discussed
the benefits and diverse applications of knowledge distillation across multiple domains,
including computer vision and natural language processing.
Iterative distillation
Our work draws significant inspiration from iterative forms of knowl-
edge distillation. Previous research often emphasized the transfer of intermediate reasoning
steps to smaller models, either sequentially (Hsieh et al., 2023; Magister et al., 2023; Shridhar
et al., 2023) or concurrently integrating various reasoning strategies (Chenglin et al., 2024;
Zhu et al., 2024; Jain et al., 2024). Notably, Wang et al. (2023) employed iterative interactions
between students and black-box teachers, with teachers providing targeted feedback based
on student failures. Similarly, Adarsh et al. (2024) combined iterative self-guided training
and self-generated data to enhance reasoning in smaller models. Additionally, Agarwal
et al. (2024) utilized on-policy training to better align student and teacher distributions by
incorporating student-generated outputs. Self-distillation methods (Zelikman et al., 2022;
Liu et al., 2024) involved models generating, filtering, and fine-tuning based on their own
rationales. Reinforcement learning-based self-training was further explored by Gulcehre
et al. (2023), where off-policy sampling informed dataset generation. Unlike these methods,
our iterative approach uniquely emphasizes a dynamic, adaptive feedback loop where the
teacher explicitly targets and rectifies the student’s learning challenges.

Methodology
In this section, we first outline the standard knowledge distillation process and discuss
its key limitations. We then introduce our proposed UNDO: UNderstanding Distillation
as Optimization framework, which addresses these limitations by enabling the teacher
to iteratively identify the student’s weaknesses and refine the knowledge being distilled
accordingly.

Experimental Details
4.1
Dataset
We utilized Numina Math dataset (LI et al., 2024) to fine-tune the student models. We
filtered out only math-word-problem ”question-type” and further did some preprocessing to
remove smaples where the answer was ”proof”, ”notfound”, empty or the problem/solution
was invalid. Since it does not include a validation set, we partitioned the training set to
create a held-out validation set. We used 20 samples sampled randomly from the training
set to create a validation set. Note that since validation set is always kept in the context
of the teacher model and grows linearly with the iterations, we kept the validation set to
fit in the context window even after K iterations. For the test set, we aggregated test sets
from various other mathematical datasets, including GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), MMLU
Pro (Wang et al., 2024), MATH 500 (Lightman et al., 2024), and SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021).
The dataset distribution is provided in Table 1. Finally, we also test our approach on two
out-of-domain commonsense reasoning datasets of StrategyQA Geva et al. (2021) (687 test
samples 1) and TheoremQA Chen et al. (2023) (800 test samples). We use 4-shot CoT for
StrategyQA and 5-shot CoT for TheoremQA evaluation.

4.2
Models
We employed llama-3.2-70B (Dubey et al., 2024) as the teacher model and selected three
student models—llama-3.3-1B, qwen-2.5-1.5B (Bai et al., 2023), and SmolLM2-1.7B (Allal
et al., 2025). The student models were chosen for their diversity in size and performance.
Across all experiments, the number of iterations (K) reached saturation at 4, meaning the
optimal results were observed at K = 3. For each student model training run, we utilized 5
epochs for the initial iteration (K = 1) and reduced it to 3 epochs for subsequent iterations.
Training each student model required approximately 10 GPU hours per epoch on H100
GPUs. Inference runs on the training data for the student models consumed approximately
240 GPU hours on GH200 GPUs, whereas generating data with the teacher model took
approximately 2,800 GPU hours (GH200s) per iteration. The temperature was set at 0.7 and
inference was done using the VLLM library (Kwon et al., 2023).

Results and Discussion
Iterative distillation improves student performance.
Table 2 presents the results of three
different student models (Qwen2.5 1.5B, SmolLM2 1.7B, and Llama-3.2 1B) over three
iterations (K) of our iterative distillation procedure UNDO. We report performance on four
mathematical reasoning datasets: GSM8K, MATH, MMLU PRO, and SVAMP, along with an Average
score across these tasks. We observe a consistent improvement over all datasets across all
models. For instance, under Qwen2.5 1.5B, the performance on GSM8K increases from
50.95 at iteration 1 to 55.04 at iteration 3. A similar trend is observed for the MATH dataset,
which improves from 32.80 →39.00, and on SVAMP which increases from 76.70 →87.40.
SmolLM2 and Llama-3.2 also follow the same upward trajectory. Notably, SmolLM2 gains
+7.40 points on MATH by iteration 3 (from 30.60 →38.00) and +2.74 points on MMLU PRO
(from 13.47 →16.21). Llama-3.2 sees gains of +9.24 and +8.40 points on GSM8K and MATH,
respectively, moving from 34.72 →43.96 on GSM8K and 20.60 →29.00 on MATH. Overall,
each model consistently benefits from iterative distillation, achieving higher accuracy on
each dataset from iteration 1 to iteration 3.
Deciding when to stop
Figure 2 visualizes the performance of Qwen2.5 and SmolLM2
across four iterations on the same four benchmarks. From this plot, we see a strong upward
trend through the first three iterations, aligning with the numerical gains reported in Table 2.
However, at iteration 4, there is a slight decrease in accuracy for both models: Qwen2.5
drops from 55.04 →53.60 on GSM8K, and SmolLM2 dips from 56.93 →55.19 on the same
dataset. Since these iteration 4 numbers are lower than iteration 3 results, we conclude
that the model has converged and additional regeneration from the teacher is no longer
beneficial, prompting us to stop further distillation steps at iteration 3.

MMLU PRO, and 72.90% on SVAMP, for an average of 39.41%—all lower than the baseline
iteration 1 model. In contrast, our proposed approach UNDO improves performance in

Does UNDO create a better teacher?
Table 3 shows that once we complete our iterative
distillation procedure and use the final teacher generated data to train student models as
standard distillation for 5 epochs, we achieve substantial gains over the baseline. Specifically,
Qwen trained on the final iteration teacher generations from a Qwen-Llama 70B interaction,
improves by more than +5 points on GSM8K (from 50.95 →56.10) and +9.80 points on
SVAMP (from 76.70 →86.50), culminating in an overall average increase of +5.52. Even
when Qwen is trained on teacher generations of a SmolLM2-Llama 70B interaction, it still
outperforms the baseline (e.g., +3.43 on average), though slightly less than with Qwen’s
own teacher data. This suggests that the teacher model—refined through UNDO—produces
data that is more personalized to the student’s weaknesses, thus effectively boosting final
performance. We also observe similar trends for the SmolLM2 model, which gains up
to +3.26 accuracy on GSM8K (from 53.60 →56.86) and +1.53 on MMLU PRO (13.47 →
15.00) when trained on the teacher data from a SmolLM2-Llama 70B interaction. Similarly,
SmolLM2 also benefits from teacher data of a Qwen-Llama 70B interaction gaining almost
+2 points on average over the baseline. The improvements indicate that the teacher–student
pair evolves into a tightly coupled relationship, outperforming a standard distillation
baseline where the teacher lacks any prior knowledge of the student’s mistakes.

Conclusion
We introduced an iterative distillation paradigm UNDO aimed at bridging the performance
gap between large teacher models and smaller student models more effectively than con-
ventional one-shot distillation. By repeatedly identifying the student’s errors, regenerating
teacher rationales that address these learning gaps, and re-training the student on this tailored
data, our method yields consistent improvements on challenging mathematical benchmarks.
Experimental results indicate that UNDO not only avoids overfitting often seen in pro-
longed standard distillation, but also makes the teacher more attuned to student-specific
weaknesses, effectively “personalizing” the teacher’s rationales.
Empirically, we observed that final teacher-generated data—after multiple rounds of itera-
tion—significantly enhances student performance, with gains of upto 20% compared to the
baseline. Even cross-teacher data (e.g., teacher data for one student used on another) shows
improved results over standard approaches. These findings underscore that incorporating

and effective transfer of reasoning knowledge.
